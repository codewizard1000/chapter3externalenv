Opening Vignette: A Storm on the Horizon for Tesla

The air in the Austin, Texas conference room was thick with the scent of whiteboard markers and ambition. It was early 2026, and Tesla's senior strategy team was gathered for its quarterly environmental scan. For years, the narrative had been one of triumphant disruption. Tesla wasn't just a car company; it was a technology juggernaut, a sustainable energy movement, and a cult brand all rolled into one. Its stock price had reflected that reality, making it the most valuable automaker on the planet by a staggering margin. But the crisp charts displayed on the 80-inch screen told a more complicated story, one where the external environment was no longer a gentle tailwind but a gathering storm.

The first slide, titled "Global Rivalry Intensifies," was a sea of logos. Ford's Mustang Mach-E and F-150 Lightning were gaining traction. GM's Ultium battery platform was finally scaling. Hyundai's Ioniq 5 and Kia's EV6 were winning critical acclaim for design and value. But the real behemoth was China's BYD. Having already surpassed Tesla in quarterly global sales, BYD was aggressively expanding into Europe and Southeast Asia with a portfolio of affordable, well-built EVs. Their vertically integrated model, which included building their own batteries, gave them a formidable cost advantage that Tesla, for all its manufacturing innovations, was struggling to match. The era of uncontested dominance was definitively over.

Next, the discussion turned to the supply chain. A map of the world lit up with choke points. Lithium from Chile, cobalt from the Democratic Republic of Congo, nickel from Indonesia. The prices of these raw materials remained volatile, subject to geopolitical whims and soaring demand not just from EV makers but from the entire electronics industry. The U.S. Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and Europe's Critical Raw Materials Act were reshaping the landscape, creating a high-stakes game of "friend-shoring" and incentivizing domestic production. But building new mines and refineries took years. Tesla's long-term battery supply, the lifeblood of its growth, was far from secure, making it vulnerable to the bargaining power of suppliers who controlled these critical inputs.

The conversation then shifted to technology and regulation. The charging standard war, once thought settled, was flaring up again. While Tesla's North American Charging Standard (NACS) had achieved widespread adoption, governments in Europe and parts of Asia were mandating the Combined Charging System (CCS). This meant bifurcated production lines and R&D spending, a classic driver of industry change. Simultaneously, regulators were scrutinizing autonomous driving technology more closely than ever. A string of high-profile accidents involving Level 2 driver-assist systems from various manufacturers had triggered public backlash and calls for stricter validation, threatening to slow down the rollout of Tesla's Full Self-Driving (FSD) software—a key differentiator and a future source of high-margin revenue.

Finally, the team looked at the consumer. Early adopters, captivated by technology and performance, had fueled the first wave of EV growth. The next wave of buyers, however, was more pragmatic. They worried about charging availability on road trips, resale values, and insurance costs, which were rising for EVs due to high repair expenses. These mainstream consumers were less loyal to Tesla's brand and more sensitive to price, making them receptive to the flood of new, cheaper models from legacy automakers. The threat of substitutes wasn't just other EVs; it was a well-made, affordable hybrid from Toyota that offered a less disruptive transition away from gasoline.

The room was quiet for a moment. The path to a million, then two million, then ten million vehicles a year had always seemed like a matter of execution—of building factories faster, designing better robots, and writing smarter code. But now, the strategy depended just as much on forces far beyond their control: the political maneuvering of global superpowers, the pricing strategies of a rival in Shenzhen, the legislative debates in Brussels, and the anxieties of a suburban family in Ohio. Their strategic degrees of freedom were being constrained not by their own limitations, but by the complex, shifting ecosystem in which they operated. Crafting a winning strategy for the next decade would require looking outward first.

3.1 From Situational Thinking to Strategy Choice

The challenges facing Tesla's leadership team are not unique. Every organization, from a multinational corporation to a local coffee shop, operates within a complex web of external forces. A brilliant product, a visionary leader, or a breakthrough technology can be rendered irrelevant if the competitive landscape shifts, consumer values change, or new regulations rewrite the rules of the game. This is why the first step in the strategy-making process is always a rigorous and honest assessment of the world outside the company's walls.

Strategic thinking begins with situational analysis. This process is typically divided into two parts: external analysis and internal analysis. This chapter is dedicated to the first half—evaluating the company's external environment. We will dissect the broader macro-environment in which the firm operates and then zoom in to analyze the structure and competitive dynamics of its specific industry. The next chapter will turn the lens inward, focusing on the company's own resources, capabilities, and competitive strengths.

As Figure 3.1 from the introductory presentation illustrates, these two streams of analysis are the essential inputs for crafting a strategic vision, identifying viable strategic options, and ultimately selecting a strategy and business model. A strategy that is not well-matched to the external environment is doomed to fail. A company that tries to be a low-cost leader in an industry where customers value prestige and customization will struggle, just as a firm that bases its strategy on open borders will suffer when protectionism rises.

Figure 3.1: The Strategic Analysis Framework

The flow from situational analysis to strategy choice. External and internal analyses converge to generate strategic options, from which the optimal strategy is selected.

The goal of this chapter is to provide you with a powerful and practical toolkit for conducting this external analysis. We will move through a logical sequence of questions, starting broad and becoming progressively more focused:

  1. What are the strategically relevant factors in the macro-environment? (PESTEL Analysis)
  2. What is the state of competition and how strong are the pressures on industry profitability? (The Five Forces)
  3. What forces are driving change in the industry, and what impact will they have? (Driving Forces Analysis)
  4. How are industry rivals positioned, and what strategic moves are they likely to make? (Strategic Group Mapping)
  5. What does it take to succeed in this industry? (Key Success Factors)
  6. How attractive is the industry in terms of its prospects for long-term profitability? (Industry Attractiveness)

By mastering these frameworks, you will be able to move beyond casual observation to a systematic diagnosis of any industry's competitive character. You will learn to identify the threats that must be mitigated and the opportunities that can be seized, laying the essential groundwork for crafting a winning strategy.

3.2 The Macro-Environment (PESTEL Analysis)

Before we can understand the specifics of competition within an industry, we must first appreciate the broader context in which that industry is situated. The macro-environment encompasses the wide-ranging environmental factors that impact all industries and all firms within them, though often in different ways and to different degrees. These are forces over which a single company typically has very little control; the only viable strategic response is to understand them, anticipate their direction, and adapt the firm's strategy to navigate the currents they create.

A popular and effective tool for organizing this analysis is the PESTEL framework. PESTEL is an acronym for six principal components of the macro-environment: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental (or Natural), and Legal. To this, we often add other relevant dimensions such as Demographics and Global forces to ensure a comprehensive scan. The purpose of PESTEL analysis is not just to list trends but to identify the handful of factors that are strategically relevant—those that have the potential to materially affect the industries and companies you are analyzing.

PESTEL Framework Visual Guide

Political, Legal, and Regulatory Factors

This dimension encompasses the full range of political, legal, and regulatory forces that can shape business activity. It includes everything from tax policy, trade regulations, and labor laws to the overall political climate and the stability of governments. For businesses, these factors define the legal and regulatory boundaries within which they must operate. A change in government can lead to a radical shift in industrial policy, as seen with the U.S. government's passage of the Inflation Reduction Act (IRA) and CHIPS Act, which created massive incentives for domestic production of semiconductors and clean energy technology. This single political action reshaped investment calculations for hundreds of firms, including Tesla and NVIDIA. Similarly, antitrust laws enforced by the Department of Justice in the U.S. or the Directorate-General for Competition in the European Union can block mergers, investigate dominant firms, and fundamentally alter industry structures. For a company like ByteDance, the political climate between the U.S. and China is not a peripheral concern; it is an existential threat that dictates where it can operate and under what conditions.

To analyze this component, managers must monitor political and legal developments at the local, national, and international levels. This involves tracking pending legislation, understanding the enforcement philosophies of different regulatory bodies, and assessing political risk in foreign markets. Evidence gathering involves reading major financial news sources (like the Wall Street Journal, Financial Times), reports from government agencies (like the Federal Trade Commission), and analysis from political risk consulting firms.

General Economic Conditions

The overall health of the economy exerts a powerful influence on nearly every industry. Key economic factors to consider include economic growth rates (GDP), interest rates, inflation rates, unemployment levels, and currency exchange rates. During periods of economic expansion, consumer confidence is high, and discretionary spending increases, benefiting industries like travel, luxury goods, and automotive. Conversely, during a recession, consumers cut back, and demand for big-ticket items plummets. Interest rates are particularly critical, as they affect the cost of capital for corporate investment and the cost of borrowing for consumers. The rapid rise in interest rates in 2022-2023, for instance, cooled the housing market and made car loans significantly more expensive, dampening demand across the automotive sector. For global companies like Red Bull, fluctuating currency exchange rates can dramatically impact the profitability of sales in foreign markets, as revenue earned in a weakening foreign currency translates into fewer dollars back home.

To gather evidence on economic conditions, strategists rely on data from central banks (like the U.S. Federal Reserve), government statistics agencies (like the Bureau of Economic Analysis), and international bodies like the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank. The key is to move beyond the headline numbers and understand the specific impact on your industry. For example, while inflation affects everyone, it disproportionately hurts companies that rely heavily on raw materials and transportation, as their costs soar.

Real-time economic indicators from global markets shape strategic decisions. Companies must monitor GDP growth, interest rates, exchange rates, and stock market movements across major economies.

Social and Cultural Forces

This dimension captures the societal values, attitudes, lifestyles, and norms that influence consumer behavior and business practices. Social forces are often slower to change than economic or technological factors, but their impact can be profound. Consider the growing consumer consciousness around health and wellness. This trend has been a headwind for traditional fast-food and sugary beverage companies while creating massive opportunities for firms in organic food, plant-based proteins, and fitness technology. Similarly, shifting attitudes toward work-life balance and remote work, accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, have reshaped commercial real estate, business travel, and software industries. For a global brand like Red Bull, which is built on a youth-oriented, high-energy lifestyle, monitoring shifts in youth culture, entertainment consumption (e.g., the rise of esports and streaming), and attitudes toward caffeine and sugar is critical for maintaining brand relevance.

Analyzing social forces requires a more qualitative approach. Strategists should pay attention to consumer surveys, market research reports from firms like Nielsen or Kantar, social media trend analysis, and cultural commentary in popular media. It's about understanding the zeitgeist—the spirit of the age—and translating that understanding into strategic insights.

Ethics & Sustainability Callout

A powerful sociocultural trend is the increasing demand for corporate social responsibility (CSR) and sustainability. Consumers, employees, and investors are no longer satisfied with companies that simply maximize profit; they expect businesses to be good stewards of the environment, to treat their workers fairly, and to contribute positively to society. This shift means that a company's environmental, social, and governance (ESG) performance is no longer a niche concern but a core component of its reputation and long-term value. Companies that ignore this trend risk alienating customers and facing difficulties in attracting top talent and capital. For industries with significant environmental footprints, such as energy or manufacturing, this social pressure is a primary driver of strategic change.

Technological Factors

Technological factors refer to the pace of technological change and the emergence of new technologies that can create or destroy entire industries. The transformative power of technology is undeniable. The rise of the internet and mobile computing has revolutionized retail, media, and banking. More recently, breakthroughs in artificial intelligence (AI), particularly generative AI, are poised to reshape knowledge work, software development, and content creation. For a company like NVIDIA, the AI boom is the single most important factor in its macro-environment, driving unprecedented demand for its specialized chips. Technological change also includes developments in materials science, biotechnology, and manufacturing processes (like 3D printing).

The strategic challenge is that technological change is often difficult to predict and can come from outside the traditional boundaries of an industry. Blockbuster's demise wasn't caused by a better video rental store; it was caused by Netflix, a tech company using a different business model. To stay ahead, companies must actively scan the technological horizon, investing in R&D, monitoring patent filings, and keeping an eye on startups and university research labs. The goal is to distinguish between fleeting fads and foundational platform shifts that will redefine the competitive landscape.

The Natural Environment / Climate

This dimension includes all factors related to the natural environment and ecological systems. For a long time, this was a minor consideration for many industries. Today, it is central. Climate change, water scarcity, extreme weather events, and the push for a circular economy are creating both risks and opportunities. Environmental factors create direct physical risks; for example, a ski resort's business model is threatened by warmer winters, and agricultural firms face risks from droughts and floods. These factors also create transition risks and opportunities related to regulation and market shifts. Stricter emissions standards for automobiles, for example, are a major regulatory force accelerating the shift to EVs. At the same time, the transition to a low-carbon economy is creating enormous markets for renewable energy, energy efficiency solutions, and sustainable materials. Every company's supply chain is vulnerable to climate-related disruptions, making environmental analysis an essential part of modern risk management.

Demographics

Demographics are the quantifiable characteristics of a population, such as its size, age distribution, geographic distribution, and income levels. These factors are powerful because they are predictable and have far-reaching consequences. For example, the aging of the population in developed countries like Japan, Germany, and the United States creates growing markets for healthcare, elder care services, and wealth management. In contrast, many emerging economies have youthful populations, which drives demand for education, entry-level housing, and consumer goods. The increasing diversity of the U.S. population creates a need for products and marketing campaigns that resonate with a wide range of cultural backgrounds. The geographic shift of population to Sun Belt states in the U.S. affects everything from real estate development to the allocation of retail stores. Because demographic data is widely available from census bureaus and government statistics agencies, it provides a solid, fact-based foundation for strategic analysis.

3.3 Dissecting the Industry and Competitive Environment: Porter's Five Forces

While PESTEL analysis provides a panoramic view of the macro-environment, we now need to zoom in on the specific arena where the company competes: its industry. An industry is a group of firms producing products or services that are close substitutes for each other. The primary goal of industry analysis is to understand the underlying structure of competition, which ultimately determines the industry's average profitability. Some industries, like software and pharmaceuticals, have historically been highly profitable. Others, like airlines and restaurants, are notoriously tough, with intense competition constantly eroding profits. Why?

The most powerful and widely used tool for diagnosing the competitive pressures in an industry is the Five Forces framework, developed by Harvard Business School professor Michael Porter. Porter's central insight was that competition is not just about the direct rivals you see every day. It is a broader contest involving five distinct forces that collectively determine the rules of engagement and the potential for profit. A strategist's job is to analyze each of these forces to understand the industry's structure and then to craft a strategy that best defends the firm against these pressures, or even influences them in the firm's favor.

The five competitive forces that shape industry profitability. The central force—rivalry among existing competitors—is influenced by the four surrounding forces.

The five forces are:

  1. The Threat of New Entrants: The risk that new competitors will enter the industry.
  2. The Bargaining Power of Buyers: The ability of customers to put the industry under pressure and demand lower prices or better quality.
  3. The Bargaining Power of Suppliers: The ability of suppliers to raise input prices or otherwise constrain firms in the industry.
  4. The Threat of Substitute Products or Services: The extent to which products or services from other industries can meet the same basic customer needs.
  5. The Intensity of Rivalry among Existing Competitors: The degree to which firms already in the industry jockey for position.

The collective strength of these five forces determines an industry's profit potential. When the forces are strong (e.g., many new entrants, powerful buyers, intense rivalry), it is difficult for any firm to earn attractive returns. When the forces are weak, the industry is structurally attractive, and profit prospects are high. The key is to assess each force systematically, identifying the specific factors that make it strong or weak.

1. The Threat of New Entrants

New entrants to an industry bring new capacity and a desire to gain market share, which puts pressure on prices, costs, and the rate of investment necessary to compete. The threat of new entrants is high when the barriers to entry are low. If it's easy for newcomers to set up shop, then any excess profits in the industry will quickly be competed away. Conversely, if entry barriers are high, incumbent firms are protected from new rivals and can enjoy higher profits for longer. Key barriers to entry include:

  • Economies of Scale: In some industries, costs per unit fall as a firm's absolute volume of production per period increases. This gives large incumbents a significant cost advantage over smaller new entrants. A new entrant faces a difficult choice: either enter on a large scale (a risky, capital-intensive bet) or enter on a small scale and accept a cost disadvantage.
  • Product Differentiation and Brand Loyalty: Existing firms may have strong brand identities and deep customer loyalty built up over years of advertising and service. A new entrant must spend heavily to overcome this inertia and build its own brand. For example, a new beverage company would find it incredibly difficult and expensive to challenge the brand power of Coca-Cola or Red Bull.
  • Capital Requirements: The need to invest large financial resources in order to compete can be a major barrier. This is particularly true in industries that require heavy spending on facilities, R&D, or upfront advertising. The EV industry, for instance, has massive capital requirements for factory construction and battery development, which is a major reason why the number of successful new entrants, like Tesla, is so small.
  • Switching Costs: These are the one-time costs buyers face when switching from one supplier's product to another. Switching costs can be monetary (e.g., breaking a contract), but they are often based on time and effort (e.g., learning a new software program) or psychological factors (e.g., the comfort of a trusted relationship). When switching costs are high, new entrants must offer a dramatic improvement in price or performance to lure customers away. NVIDIA's CUDA software platform creates high switching costs for AI developers who have invested years in learning the ecosystem, making it difficult for competing chipmakers to gain a foothold.
  • Access to Distribution Channels: A new entrant needs to secure distribution for its product. The existing competitors may have tied up the most effective distribution channels, making it difficult for newcomers to get their products onto retail shelves or into the hands of customers.
  • Cost Advantages Independent of Scale: Incumbent firms may have cost advantages that are difficult for rivals to replicate, such as proprietary technology, access to the best raw material sources, favorable geographic locations, or experience curve effects (where costs decline as a firm gains cumulative experience in production).
  • Government Policy: Governments can limit or even foreclose entry to industries with such policies as licensing requirements, restrictions on foreign investment, or extensive safety and environmental regulations.

Mini-Example: Tesla and Rivalry/Entrants. In the early 2010s, the entry barriers into the auto industry were considered almost insurmountable due to massive capital requirements and economies of scale. Tesla overcame these by using a novel direct-to-consumer model and focusing on a high-end niche first. Today, Tesla itself benefits from high entry barriers. Its giga-factories provide scale economies, its Supercharger network created switching costs (at least initially), and its brand is incredibly strong. However, the rise of well-capitalized new entrants from China (like BYD) and the pivot by legacy automakers (like Ford and GM) show that even formidable barriers can be overcome, intensifying rivalry across the entire EV segment. This leads to price wars, increased advertising spending, and pressure on everyone's profit margins.

2. The Bargaining Power of Buyers

Powerful customers—the "buyers"—can capture more value by forcing down prices, demanding better quality or more service (which drives up costs), and generally playing industry participants off against one another. The bargaining power of buyers is high under several conditions:

  • They are concentrated or purchase in large volumes. If a few large buyers account for a big slice of an industry's sales, they have more clout. Walmart, for example, is a powerful buyer for many consumer goods manufacturers because it purchases in enormous quantities.
  • The industry's products are standardized or undifferentiated. If buyers believe they are buying a commodity, they are more willing to switch between vendors based on price. This is common in industries like basic chemicals or agricultural products.
  • They face few switching costs. As discussed earlier, if it's easy for buyers to switch, they can and will, which keeps sellers honest on price and quality.
  • They can credibly threaten to integrate backward. If buyers can plausibly threaten to produce the industry's product themselves, they have more leverage in negotiations. Automakers, for instance, often use the threat of producing components in-house to bargain down prices from their suppliers.
  • They are well-informed. The more information buyers have about sellers' products, prices, and costs, the better they can bargain. The internet has dramatically increased buyer power in many industries by making price comparisons easy and transparent.

Mini-Example: Red Bull and Buyer Power. Red Bull faces significant buyer power from large retailers like Walmart, Costco, and major supermarket chains. These retailers are highly concentrated and purchase in massive volumes. They can demand favorable pricing, promotional support, and premium shelf space. The threat is that if Red Bull doesn't comply, the retailer could dedicate more space to rivals like Monster or even its own private-label energy drink. Red Bull counters this power by investing billions in global marketing and sponsorships (like Formula 1 racing) to create immense consumer pull. The goal is to make its brand so strong that consumers demand it by name, forcing retailers to stock it regardless of the terms.

3. The Bargaining Power of Suppliers

Powerful suppliers can capture more value by charging higher prices, limiting the quality or quantity of the goods and services they provide, or shifting costs to industry participants. The bargaining power of suppliers is high when:

  • The supplier group is more concentrated than the industry it sells to. If an industry relies on a few powerful suppliers, those suppliers have more leverage. The PC industry's reliance on Microsoft (for operating systems) and Intel (for microprocessors) in the 1990s is a classic example.
  • The supplier group does not depend heavily on the industry for its revenues. If suppliers sell to many different industries, they don't have to be overly accommodating to any single one.
  • Industry participants face high switching costs in changing suppliers. This might be because a supplier's product is unique or because a manufacturer has integrated the supplier's part deeply into its production process.
  • The suppliers offer products that are differentiated. If a supplier's product is critical and has few or no substitutes, it has more power.
  • There is no substitute for what the supplier group provides. This gives suppliers a great deal of pricing power.
  • The supplier group can credibly threaten to integrate forward. If suppliers can threaten to enter the buyer's industry, they can command better terms.

Mini-Example: NVIDIA and Supplier/Substitute Threats. NVIDIA is a dominant firm, but it is highly dependent on a single key supplier: Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC). TSMC operates the world's most advanced semiconductor foundries, and producing cutting-edge AI chips requires access to its latest process nodes. This gives TSMC immense bargaining power. NVIDIA can't easily switch to another foundry for its highest-performance chips, and TSMC serves a wide range of customers (like Apple and AMD), so it doesn't depend solely on NVIDIA. This supplier concentration is a major strategic vulnerability. In terms of substitutes, while NVIDIA's GPUs are the gold standard for AI, large customers like Google, Amazon, and Microsoft are developing their own custom AI accelerator chips. These in-house chips represent a significant threat of substitution, aimed at reducing their dependence on NVIDIA and lowering their costs over the long run.

4. The Threat of Substitute Products or Services

A substitute performs the same or a similar function as an industry's product by a different means. For example, video conferencing is a substitute for business travel. Sweeteners like stevia are substitutes for sugar. A train is a substitute for a short-haul flight. The threat of substitutes is high when:

  • The substitute offers an attractive price-performance trade-off. The substitute doesn't have to be better on all dimensions. If it's significantly cheaper and "good enough" on performance, it can place a strong ceiling on the prices an industry can charge. The rise of free, ad-supported streaming services (like YouTube) is a powerful substitute for traditional paid cable television.
  • The buyer's cost of switching to the substitute is low. The easier it is for customers to switch, the greater the competitive pressure from substitutes.

Strategists must be careful not to define their industry too narrowly, which can cause them to miss threats from substitutes. The railroad industry in the early 20th century saw itself as being in the "railroad business" instead of the "transportation business," and was subsequently blindsided by the rise of trucks and airplanes.

Mini-Example: ByteDance and Substitutes. TikTok's primary rivals are other short-form video apps like Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts. But the more profound competitive threat comes from substitutes for its core function: capturing users' attention. In this "attention market," TikTok competes with a vast array of substitutes. These include video games (Fortnite), streaming services (Netflix), music (Spotify), and even sleep. Any activity that occupies a user's free time is a substitute. This places a constant pressure on ByteDance to innovate and make its algorithm ever more engaging to prevent users from switching their attention—and the advertising dollars that follow it—to another platform.

5. The Intensity of Rivalry among Existing Competitors

This force is often the most visible. Rivalry among existing competitors takes familiar forms, including price discounting, new product introductions, advertising campaigns, and service improvements. High rivalry limits the profitability of an industry. The intensity of rivalry is greatest if:

  • Competitors are numerous or are roughly equal in size and power. When many firms are vying for leadership, or when a few firms of similar strength are locked in a battle for dominance, the competitive environment is unstable and prone to intense conflict.
  • Industry growth is slow. Slow growth turns competition into a zero-sum game, as firms can only grow by stealing market share from one another.
  • Exit barriers are high. Exit barriers are economic, strategic, and emotional factors that keep companies competing even though they may be earning low or negative returns. These can include specialized assets that are hard to sell, high fixed costs of exit (like severance pay), or a manager's emotional attachment to a business. When firms are trapped in an industry, they are often forced to compete more aggressively.
  • The product or service is a commodity with few switching costs. This encourages firms to compete on price, which is often the most destructive form of competition.
  • Fixed costs are high and marginal costs are low. This creates a powerful temptation for firms to cut prices to fill any unused capacity. The airline industry is a classic example; the cost of adding one more passenger to a flight is minimal, so airlines are constantly tempted to slash fares to ensure planes fly full.
  • The rivals are diverse in their strategies, origins, and "personalities." A group of competitors that differ in their objectives and approaches can have a hard time coordinating or "reading" each other's moves, leading to more frequent and intense clashes.

For example, in the coffee shop industry, the threat of new entrants is very strong (low barriers to entry), and rivalry is intense. This helps explain why it's a tough business with many closures.

3.4 Drivers of Industry Change

Industry structures are not static; they are constantly evolving. The Five Forces analysis provides a snapshot of the competitive landscape at a single point in time. However, a strategist must also understand the forces that are driving change in that landscape. Drivers of change are the major underlying causes of change in industry and competitive conditions. By identifying them, managers can better anticipate how the industry is likely to evolve and what strategic adjustments will be needed.

The analysis of driving forces has three main steps:

  1. Identify the most important drivers of change.
  2. Forecast the direction and potential impact of each driver. Are they acting to make the industry more or less attractive?
  3. Translate the forecast into strategic moves. What actions should the firm take now to prepare for the changes ahead?

While there are dozens of potential drivers, they usually originate in the PESTEL macro-environment. Some of the most common and powerful drivers include:

  • Changes in the Long-Term Industry Growth Rate: A slowdown in industry growth is a powerful driver for increased rivalry, as firms battle for a smaller pool of new customers. Conversely, accelerating growth can dampen rivalry and create opportunities for all participants.
  • Increasing Globalization: Globalization can drive change by opening up new markets, increasing the number of foreign competitors, and shifting production and value chain activities across borders.
  • Technological Change and New Internet Capabilities: As we saw with PESTEL, technological change can be a massive driver. The emergence of the internet, for example, fundamentally changed the music, travel, and retail industries by creating new business models and distribution channels.
  • Product and Marketing Innovation: When firms introduce new products or new ways of marketing, it can shift buyer preferences and reshape competition. Apple's introduction of the iPhone was a product innovation that transformed the entire mobile phone industry.
  • Entry or Exit of Major Firms: The entry of a significant new player (like Google entering the cloud computing market) or the exit of a major incumbent can shake up an industry's structure and competitive dynamics.
  • Diffusion of Technical Know-How: As knowledge and technology spread, what was once a source of competitive advantage for a few firms can become a common capability, leveling the playing field.
  • Changes in Cost and Efficiency: Breakthroughs in process technology or business models can dramatically lower costs, forcing all firms in the industry to adapt. The rise of hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") in the energy sector is a prime example.
  • Regulatory Influences and Government Policy Changes: New laws and regulations can be powerful drivers of change. Deregulation in the airline and telecommunications industries spurred decades of intense competition. Today, environmental regulations are a key driver in the automotive and energy sectors.
  • Changing Societal Concerns, Attitudes, and Lifestyles: Shifts in social values, as discussed in the PESTEL section, can drive significant change. The move toward healthier eating has been a major driver of change in the food and beverage industry.

Once the key drivers have been identified, the second step is to assess their impact. Will they cause the Five Forces to become stronger or weaker? Will they increase or decrease demand for the industry's product? Will they raise or lower the barriers to entry? The goal is to determine whether the collective impact of the drivers will make the industry more or less attractive for profitability.

The final step is to translate this analysis into action. The insights from driving forces analysis can help a company decide what strategic moves to make. This might involve investing in new technologies, shifting geographic focus, or changing the business model. It allows managers to think proactively, categorizing potential responses into a simple framework: what must we do now, what should we avoid now, and what do we need to prepare for later?

3.5 Strategic Group Mapping

The Five Forces and Drivers of Change analyses help us understand the industry as a whole. However, competition is often most intense between firms that are most similar to one another. A strategic group is a cluster of industry rivals that have similar competitive approaches and market positions. For example, in the automotive industry, Ferrari and Lamborghini compete with each other, but not directly with Toyota or Honda. They are in a different strategic group.

Strategic group mapping is a technique for displaying the different market or competitive positions that rival firms occupy in the industry. It's a useful tool for understanding the structure of competition and for identifying a firm's closest rivals.

Constructing a strategic group map involves these steps:

  1. Identify the competitive characteristics that differentiate firms in the industry. The key is to choose variables that capture the most important strategic differences. Common variables include price/quality range (high, medium, low), geographic coverage (local, regional, national, global), product-line breadth (wide, narrow), degree of vertical integration, or choice of distribution channels.
  2. Plot the firms on a two-variable map using pairs of these differentiating characteristics. The axes of the map should not be highly correlated. For example, plotting firms on axes of price and quality would be unhelpful if high price always corresponds with high quality.
  3. Assign firms that fall in about the same strategy space to the same strategic group.
  4. Draw circles around each strategic group, making the circles proportional to the size of the group's share of total industry sales revenue. This last step helps to visualize the relative importance of each strategic approach in the industry.

What the map reveals is invaluable. First, it shows which firms are the closest competitors. Rivalry is typically strongest within a strategic group. Second, it can reveal "white space"—unoccupied positions on the map that might represent attractive strategic opportunities. A company could potentially create a new strategic group by pioneering a novel competitive approach. Third, the map helps to explain why profit potential can differ across strategic groups. The competitive pressures of the five forces may not be uniform across the industry; they often impact different strategic groups in different ways. For example, the threat of new entrants might be low in the high-end, brand-focused strategic group, but high in the low-price, private-label group. Finally, strategic group analysis helps in predicting the likely rival moves. Firms in the same group are under similar market pressures and are likely to respond to industry changes in similar ways. It also highlights the threat of firms in adjacent groups attempting to move into your group.

3.6 Key Success Factors (KSFs) & Industry Attractiveness

After analyzing the industry's structure, drivers of change, and competitive positioning, we can distill our findings into a concise set of Key Success Factors. KSFs are the strategy elements, product attributes, competitive capabilities, or intangible assets with the greatest impact on future competitive success in the marketplace. They are the things that a company must get right to survive and thrive. Answering the question, "What do we need to be good at?" is the essence of identifying KSFs.

KSFs can be derived directly from our previous analysis. They are the factors that arise from the workings of the macro-environment and the five competitive forces. For example:

  • In an industry with intense rivalry and price-based competition, a key success factor will likely be a relentless focus on low costs.
  • In an industry where buyer power is high and products are commodities, a KSF might be building a strong brand to differentiate the product.
  • In an industry driven by rapid technological change, KSFs will revolve around R&D, innovation speed, and the ability to attract top engineering talent.

Typically, an industry will have three to five KSFs. Any more than that suggests the analysis is not yet focused on what is truly critical. Identifying them is a crucial step in linking our external analysis to strategic action. A firm's strategy must be built around mastering the KSFs of its industry.

Evaluating Industry Attractiveness

The final step in our external analysis is to synthesize everything we have learned to make an overall assessment of industry attractiveness. Is this industry a good place to be? Does it offer strong prospects for attractive, long-term profits? This evaluation is not just about the industry's growth rate. A fast-growing industry can be unattractive if it is structurally flawed with strong competitive forces.

The key factors to consider in judging industry attractiveness are:

  1. The industry's growth potential.
  2. The collective strength of the Five Forces. Are they strong enough to undermine profitability?
  3. The impact of the driving forces. Are they acting to make the industry more or less attractive in the future?
  4. The potential for profitability. Does the industry structure allow for good margins, or is it a "commodity hell" where profits are constantly competed away?

It's also important to look beyond industry averages. The presence of complementors—companies that sell products or services that enhance the value of the industry's product (like apps for a smartphone)—can increase the overall value pool. The industry's stage in its life cycle (emerging, growing, mature, declining) also has a major bearing on its attractiveness. Ultimately, the goal is to determine whether the industry presents a company with a good opportunity for profit, given the company's own particular resources and capabilities (which we will discuss in the next chapter).

4. International Business Foundations

In today's interconnected world, a purely domestic external analysis is insufficient for most businesses. The forces of globalization—the growing integration and interdependence of the world's economies—mean that opportunities and threats can emerge from anywhere on the planet. Therefore, we must extend our analytical toolkit to evaluate the global external environment.

Global supply chains connect production and consumption across continents. A single product may source materials from dozens of countries, be assembled in another, and sold worldwide.

Globalization Dynamics

Globalization has two primary facets: the globalization of markets and the globalization of production. The globalization of markets refers to the merging of historically distinct and separate national markets into one huge global marketplace. Falling trade barriers and converging consumer tastes have made this possible. The globalization of production refers to the sourcing of goods and services from locations around the globe to take advantage of national differences in the cost and quality of factors of production (like labor, energy, land, and capital). This has led to the creation of complex global value chains, where different stages of the production process are located in different countries. For example, an iPhone is designed in California, sources components from dozens of countries, and is assembled in China.

In recent years, the unquestioned march of globalization has faced headwinds. Geopolitical tensions have led to a rise in protectionism and trade disputes, prompting companies to rethink their sprawling supply chains. This has given rise to trends like near-shoring (moving production closer to home markets) and friend-shoring (moving production to allied countries) to reduce political risk. For a company like NVIDIA, U.S. government export controls on advanced chips to China represent a massive intervention in its global strategy, cutting it off from a major market.

The Role of Institutions

When operating internationally, firms must navigate a different set of formal and informal institutions. Formal institutions include the laws, regulations, and legal systems in a given country. They also include key global organizations that set the rules for international trade and finance, such as the World Trade Organization (WTO), which polices the world trading system; the International Monetary Fund (IMF), which maintains order in the international monetary system; and the World Bank, which promotes economic development. Regional trade blocs, like the European Union (EU) or the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA), create their own sets of rules that govern commerce within the bloc. For ByteDance, the EU's Digital Services Act (DSA) and Digital Markets Act (DMA) represent a powerful institutional force that dictates how it must moderate content and manage its platform in its European markets.

Country Risk and Entry Modes

Every country presents a unique set of risks and opportunities. Country risk is a broad concept that includes:

  • Political Risk: The likelihood that political forces will cause drastic changes in a country's business environment that adversely affect the profit and other goals of a business enterprise. This can range from social unrest and terrorism to nationalization of assets.
  • Economic Risk: The likelihood that economic mismanagement will cause drastic changes, such as hyperinflation or a sovereign debt default.
  • Legal Risk: The likelihood that a trading partner will opportunistically break a contract or expropriate intellectual property rights. The strength of IP enforcement varies dramatically between countries and is a major concern for technology and pharmaceutical companies.

Once a company decides to enter a foreign market, it must choose an entry mode. The choice involves a trade-off between the level of commitment (and risk) and the degree of control.

Mini-Cases

Mini-Case 1: ByteDance/TikTok: Platform Growth under Geopolitical Headwinds

TikTok's meteoric rise was fueled by a powerful combination of a hyper-engaging algorithm and strong network effects—the more users who joined, the more valuable the platform became for both creators and other users. By 2025, it was a dominant force in global culture. However, its Chinese parent company, ByteDance, placed it directly in the crosshairs of U.S.-China geopolitical rivalry. Citing national security concerns, U.S. lawmakers passed legislation forcing ByteDance to either sell TikTok's U.S. operations or face a nationwide ban. Similar scrutiny was rising in Europe over data governance and the platform's impact on young users.

This regulatory pressure represented a fundamental threat to TikTok's business model. A ban in the U.S., its most lucrative market, would be devastating. A forced sale would separate it from ByteDance's engineering talent and core algorithmic know-how. Meanwhile, the threat from substitutes remained intense. Meta's Instagram Reels and Google's YouTube Shorts were formidable competitors, backed by massive user bases and deep advertising relationships. They were quick to capitalize on any uncertainty surrounding TikTok, courting creators and running campaigns to lure away users. Internally, TikTok had to navigate the delicate balance of complying with Western data localization laws (requiring U.S. user data to be stored on U.S. soil) while not appearing to betray its Chinese roots, a tightrope walk that consumed immense legal and financial resources. The platform that had mastered the art of viral trends was now subject to the much more powerful and unpredictable force of global politics.

Mini-Case 2: Tesla: EV Rivalry and Entry Barriers in 2026

By 2026, the electric vehicle market looked nothing like the open field Tesla had once dominated. Every major automaker had launched multiple EV models, and the intensity of rivalry had reached a fever pitch. Price wars, once unthinkable, were now common as companies fought for market share to utilize their massive factory investments. The key drivers of change were shifting. While battery range and performance were still important, the new battlegrounds were manufacturing cost, software experience, and charging convenience. Tesla's primary entry barriers—its proprietary Supercharger network and its lead in battery technology—were eroding. Many automakers had adopted Tesla's NACS charging standard, granting their customers access to the Supercharger network and neutralizing a key advantage. On the battery front, competitors like BYD were producing low-cost LFP batteries at a massive scale, while others were making progress on solid-state battery technology, which promised a future leap in performance. The capital cycles were brutal; building a new battery plant or vehicle assembly line required billions of dollars and years of planning, meaning that strategic bets had to be made far in advance of market shifts. The Key Success Factors in the industry were evolving from simply making a compelling EV to making a compelling EV profitably at scale.

Mini-Case 3: NVIDIA: AI Accelerators, Ecosystems, and Supplier Dependence

NVIDIA's position in the AI boom of the mid-2020s seemed unassailable. Its GPUs had become the default hardware for training large language models, giving it a market share of over 90% in the AI data center segment. This dominance, however, was not just about hardware. The true source of its competitive advantage—its "moat"—was CUDA, its proprietary software development platform. Millions of developers had invested years learning to write code for NVIDIA's chips using CUDA. This created enormous switching costs, making it difficult for competitors like AMD or Intel to peel away customers, even if they offered comparable hardware. NVIDIA's business model relied on this hardware-software ecosystem, with the CUDA platform acting as a powerful complement that locked in customers.

However, NVIDIA's position came with two major vulnerabilities. First was its extreme dependence on a single supplier, TSMC, for manufacturing its most advanced chips. Any disruption to TSMC's production—whether from geopolitical events surrounding Taiwan or simple capacity constraints—would be catastrophic for NVIDIA. This gave TSMC immense bargaining power. The second vulnerability came from its largest customers. Cloud providers like Amazon (AWS), Microsoft (Azure), and Google (GCP) were both NVIDIA's biggest buyers and its potential future competitors. They were all investing billions to design their own custom AI chips (e.g., Google's TPU, Amazon's Inferentia) to optimize performance for their specific workloads and, crucially, to reduce their long-term dependence on NVIDIA and its high-margin products.

Mini-Case 4: Red Bull: Defending a Global Brand in a Crowded Market

For over three decades, Red Bull GmbH perfected a formula for market dominance: sell a single, premium-priced product—its iconic energy drink—and promote it through a brilliant and expensive global marketing machine centered on extreme sports and spectacular events. This strategy built an incredibly powerful global brand, allowing Red Bull to command a price premium over dozens of copycat threats and private-label competitors. Its key asset was not its recipe but its brand image, which resonated with a core demographic of young, active consumers.

By the mid-2020s, however, the landscape was shifting. The primary driver of change was a growing socio-cultural and regulatory focus on health and wellness. Governments around the world were implementing or considering "sugar taxes" and restrictions on the marketing of high-caffeine beverages to minors. This directly threatened Red Bull's core product. Furthermore, buyer power, particularly from large supermarket and convenience store chains, remained high. These retailers controlled access to the consumer and were constantly pushing for better margins. The competitive space was also becoming more crowded. Monster Beverage Corp. was a fierce rival with a broader product line. Meanwhile, a new strategic group of "performance energy" drinks was emerging, with brands like Celsius and C4 targeting fitness enthusiasts with claims of metabolic benefits, blurring the lines between energy drinks, sports drinks, and supplements. Red Bull's focused strategy, once a source of strength, was now being challenged by multiple external forces.

Chapter Summary

This chapter has provided a comprehensive framework for conducting an external analysis, a critical first step in the strategic management process. The goal is to develop a deep and nuanced understanding of the world outside the firm's walls in order to craft a strategy that is well-aligned with environmental realities.

We began at the broadest level with PESTEL analysis, which helps to identify the key Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal factors in the macro-environment. These are the large-scale forces that shape the context for all industries.

From there, we zoomed in to the competitive arena using Porter's Five Forces framework. This powerful tool helps us to diagnose the underlying structure of an industry by analyzing the threat of new entrants, the bargaining power of buyers and suppliers, the threat of substitutes, and the intensity of rivalry among existing firms. The collective strength of these forces determines the industry's average profit potential and overall attractiveness.

Because industries are not static, we then introduced the concept of drivers of change. By identifying the forces that are causing an industry to evolve—such as technological shifts, globalization, or new regulations—managers can anticipate the future competitive landscape and proactively adjust their strategies.

To understand the competitive landscape in more detail, we used strategic group mapping. This tool allows us to visualize the different competitive positions of rivals and to identify a firm's closest competitors, revealing that rivalry is often most intense between firms with similar strategies.

Finally, we synthesized these analyses to identify an industry's Key Success Factors (KSFs)—the critical elements necessary for competitive success—and to make an overall assessment of industry attractiveness. We also extended our analysis to the global context, examining the dynamics of globalization, the role of institutions, country risk, and the critical choice of foreign market entry modes.

Together, these tools—PESTEL, Five Forces, Drivers of Change, Strategic Group Mapping, and KSFs—form a logical chain of inquiry. They allow a manager to move from a high-level environmental scan to a detailed, actionable understanding of their industry's competitive character. This external analysis is the essential foundation upon which a sound strategy is built.

Index of Key Terms

Bargaining Power of Buyers: The ability of customers to pressure firms by demanding lower prices or higher quality, thereby capturing more value for themselves.
Bargaining Power of Suppliers: The ability of suppliers to pressure firms by raising prices or reducing the quality of purchased goods or services.
Barriers to Entry: Obstacles that make it difficult for a new firm to enter an industry, such as economies of scale, brand loyalty, or capital requirements.
Complementors: Companies or products that enhance the value of another company's product. For example, apps are complementors to smartphones.
Country Risk: The exposure to potential loss or adverse effects on company operations and profitability caused by a country's political, economic, or legal developments.
Drivers of Change: The major underlying causes of change in industry and competitive conditions.
Entry Modes: The various methods for entering a foreign market, such as exporting, licensing, joint ventures, or direct investment.
Five Forces: A framework developed by Michael Porter for analyzing the structure of an industry and its profitability, consisting of the threat of new entrants, buyer power, supplier power, the threat of substitutes, and the intensity of rivalry.
Globalization: The process of increasing integration and interdependence between national economies.
Industry Attractiveness: An assessment of an industry's long-term profit potential based on a full analysis of its structure and competitive dynamics.
Key Success Factors (KSFs): The product attributes, operational approaches, resources, and competitive capabilities that are necessary for a firm to compete successfully in an industry.
Macro-environment: The broad environmental context in which a company's industry is situated; it includes politically, economically, socially, technologically, environmentally, and legally relevant factors.
Network Effects: The phenomenon whereby a product or service becomes more valuable as more people use it.
PESTEL Analysis: A framework for scanning the macro-environment for strategically relevant factors: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Environmental, and Legal.
Strategic Group: A cluster of industry rivals that have similar competitive approaches and market positions.
Switching Costs: The one-time costs that buyers face when switching from one supplier's product to another.
